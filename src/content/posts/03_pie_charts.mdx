  &emsp; Many kids start learning about graphs in elementary school. Bar graphs, line graphs, pictograms and pie charts are the usual suspects. They aren't hard to understand, even for children. But our graphical education doesn't end there. Obviously, people who studied statistics will be familiar with box plots and scatter plots, but graphs are prevalent in all fields. MBA pursuers may have learned about a funnel chart, aeronautics enthusiasts should know the wind rose diagram, public health officials constantly wrangle with geographic charts, and so on. We are visual creatures, and <b>well-made</b> graphs make complicated data easier to comprehend by aggregating and summarizing like groups for our brains. 
  
  For example, below is a table of the most streamed spotify artists, obtained from a [Kaggle dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/srisyra02/spotify-music-artist-streaming-analytics) about Spotify's top streamed artists. Imagine we want to analyze the total streams. To be fair, the data is already processed and organized, so it's not too hard. In fact, we can simply read in order to see that Drake is the most streamed artist, Taylor Swift is the second most streamed, and so on.

But what if we want to see the most streamed genres? Instead of pulling out a calculator and adding streams row by row, we can invoke the humble barchart. When using [`ggplot2`](https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/reference/geom_bar.html) and [`dplyr`](https://dplyr.tidyverse.org/), the package does all of the arithmetic and coding for us!
<div class = "flex">
  ```r
  genre <- df |>
  group_by(primary_genre) |>
  summarise(
    total_streams_in_millions = sum(total_streams_in_millions)
  ) |>
  top_n(7, total_streams_in_millions)


  ggplot(genre, aes(x = reorder(primary_genre, -total_streams_in_millions), 
                  y = total_streams_in_millions, 
                  fill = primary_genre)) + 
  geom_col() + 
  labs(
    x = "Genre",
    y = "Total Streams (in millions)"
  ) +
  guides(fill = "none") + 
  scale_y_continuous(labels = scales::label_comma()) + 
  theme_minimal()
  ```

The bar chart is pretty straightforward: the taller the bar, the more streams a genre has. In fact, the other graphs have intuitive interpretations as well, so what's the deal with pie charts? After all, the size of a slice in a pie chart is analgous to the height of a bar in a bar graph. 

  
<h2> The History of Graphs </h2>

  &emsp; Graphs could have been useful to many people throughout history. When the Ancient Mesopotamians recorded the first bartering system, they could have created a pictograph to keep track of trades. When the Ancient Greeks made advances in geoemetry, they could have used a pie chart to represent pieces of a whole. But here is the thing: graphs are abstract. Take the above example: Looking at the graph, we are able to see clearly that Hip-Hop had the most streams, followed by Pop and Reggaeton. However, we are only able to glean this information because of context clues: the x axis, the y axis, and the category labels. But if we remove the context, the bar graph could easily explain trends in other data. Maybe the different sized bars represent the daily bus traffic volume or maybe they represent the most ordered flavors in an ice cream shop. Graphs <b>strip awaay physical details to focus entirely on relationships,</b> and relationships in data is abstract and sometimes monstrous.
