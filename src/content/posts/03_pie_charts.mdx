MBA students learn about funnel charts, aeronautics enthusiasts use wind rose diagrams, and public health officials constantly wrangle with geographic charts. The bar chart is pretty straightforward: the taller the bar, the more streams a genre has. In fact, the other graphs have intuitive interpretations as well, so what's the deal with pie charts? After all, the size of a slice in a pie chart is analogous to the height of a bar in a bar graph. 

  
<h2> The History of Graphs </h2>

  &emsp; Throughout history, humans could have relied on visual aids to organize complex information. For instance, when the Ancient Mesopotamians recorded their first bartering system, they might have used pictographs to keep track of trades. When the Ancient Greeks made advances in geometry, they could have used a pie chart to represent pieces of a whole. But they didn't. 
  
  Why? Because graphs are abstract! In the previous example, looking at the bar chart clearly shows that Hip-Hop had the most streams, followed by Pop and Reggaeton. However, we are only able to glean this information because of context clues: the x-axis, the y-axis, and the category labels. But if we remove the context, the bar graph could easily explain trends in other data. Maybe the different-sized bars represent the daily bus traffic volume or maybe they represent the most ordered flavors in an ice cream shop. Graphs <b>strip away physical details to focus entirely on relationships,</b> and relationships in data are abstract and sometimes unwieldy.

    As scientists made advances, graphs naturally advanced as well. In 1801, William Playfair published <u><b>Statistical Breviary</b></u>[^4], in which he introduced the first pie chart. Now, it was a fairly simple chart, but it perfectly displays the capabilities of pie charts: given a whole, each slice represents the relative size of a component. At the time of Playfair's writing, the Turkish empire spanned 790,000 square miles. From the chart, it is clear that most of the land lies in Asia, followed by Europe and Africa.
    
    
    Following the pie charts, other circle graphs were formalized. In 1877, Georg von Mayr released <b><u>Die Gesetzmässigkeit im Gesellschaftsleben: Statistische Studien</u></b> (The Regularities in Social Life: Statistical Studies) where he presented a circle graph, which he called "Linien-Diagramme im Kreise" (line chart in a circle) [^5]. This is an early example of a radar chart, which allows for easier comparison of multiple quantitative variables across the same groups. 
    
<h2> Finally, What's My Deal with Pie Charts? </h2>

My biggest gripe with pie charts is that they can be hard to read, and I'm not the only one who thinks so [^6]. Sure, if there are only three categories with distinctly different sizes as in the William Playfair example, then a pie chart is perfectly fine. However, when dealing with multiple categories that have only minor differences in size, a bar chart represents the data much more clearly.


For example, take the Spotify data above and re-visualize it as a pie chart. 

<img class ="img3" src = {`${import.meta.env.BASE_URL}/02_graphs/pie-genre.jpg`}/>

The chart has ordered the slices from highest to lowest number of streams, but that is not immediately clear. The Hip-Hop and Pop wedges are too similar in size, as are the R&B, Latin and EDM slices. If the visualization goal was to display the total number of streams per genre, the bar graph shows the stream differences better. 

However, if the goal was to display the total number of streams in percentages then the pie chart is better than the bar graph, but there are still two problems. First, in the current pie chart, Hip-Hop appears to make up roughly 30% of the total streams. However, this is misleading because the chart doesn't represent the true whole &mdash; it only accounts for the Top 7 genres and not all 23.


Second, even if we do include all genres, a pie chart with many slices is unwieldy to label and read. Sure, we can create a separate category called "Other," but that may compress too much information depending on how many genres are counted as an "other." Moreover, if we include that many slices, it may be necessary to start labelling percentages, but at that point, the pie chart is no better than a table. So, when comparing percentages, I typically prefer a waffle chart: 

<div class = "flex">
  ```r
  total_streams <- sum(df$total_streams_in_millions)
n_squares <- 100

hip_hop_streams <- sum(df %>% filter(primary_genre == "Hip-Hop") %>% select(total_streams_in_millions))
pop_streams <- sum(df %>% filter(primary_genre == "Pop") %>% select(total_streams_in_millions))
reggaeton <- sum(df %>% filter(primary_genre == "Reggaeton") %>% select(total_streams_in_millions))

hip_hop_pct <- round(hip_hop_streams / total_streams * n_squares)
pop_pct <- round(pop_streams / total_streams * n_squares)
reggaeton_pct <- round(reggaeton / total_streams * n_squares)


# Transforming into a data frame

waffle_data <- data.frame(
  pos = 1:n_squares,
  category = c(rep("Hip-Hop", hip_hop_pct), rep("Pop", pop_pct), rep("Reggaeton",reggaeton_pct), rep("Other", n_squares - hip_hop_pct - pop_pct - reggaeton_pct))
) %>%
  mutate(
    x = (pos - 1) %% 10,
    y = (pos - 1) %/% 10
  )


waffle <- ggplot(waffle_data, aes(x = x, y = y, fill = category)) +
  geom_tile(color = "white", linewidth = 1, width = 0.9, height = 0.9) +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("Hip-Hop" = "#145214", "Pop" = "#C75C5C", "Reggaeton" = "#228B22", "Other" = "#F7E8E6")) +
  coord_equal() +
  scale_y_reverse() +
  theme_void() +
  labs(
    title = "Top 3 Genre Percentages",
    fill = NULL
  ) +
  theme(
    plot.title = element_text(face = "bold", size = 14, hjust = .5),
    legend.position = "bottom"
  )
  ```

<h2> Concluding Thoughts </h2>

Although I tend to gravitate towards other kinds of graphs, I do think pie charts still have their uses.

<ol>
  <li> If we are only comparing two to three groups, then a pie chart can be a good visualization tool, especially if the differences are large. </li> 
  <li> Pie charts can be especially useful for tracking some sort of percentage-related goal. For example, if I am tracking my budget and want to keep rent under 30% (following the traditional rule of thumb), then a pie chart can be a quick visualization to see how much of my money is going towards rent. </li>
  <li> In general, a pie chart can be good if we don't care about exact percentages but only care about how one category relates to another. Going back to budgeting, if all I care about is that my eating-out budget is much lower than my grocery budget for a month, then a pie chart can help me quickly see the breakdown.</li>
  <li> Finally, almost everyone has learned about pie charts during school. This means that the pie chart is one of the more friendly charts to read and interpret for the <b>general public</b>. During my time in academia, I became accustomed to presenting visualizations to those who are super data-savvy; however, most readers may not understand more complicated graphs. </li>
</ol>
