  &emsp; In 1989, the Democratic congress led by Charles R. McDonald teamed with Republican businessman Patrick Taylor in a rare case of bipartisanship to push the
"Taylor Plan", or the Taylor Opportunity Program for Students (TOPS) as it is known today. The plan was simple: to make education more affordable for Louisiana's brightest
by waiving tuition for high-achieving students. The hope was that intelligent high schoolers would become more intelligent college students (collegers?), who would later 
bring that intelligence into the Louisiana workforce. While TOPS today works similarily to back then, the tapestry of Louisiana has changed rapidly since the plan's inception.

For one, the scholarship is no longer funded by the Taylor Foundation. While states like Georgia fund in-state scholarships via the state's lottery system, TOPS is completely 
funded by the government, costing around 270$ million per year. Which that price tag, many congressman has called into question the return on investment in Louisiana's 
elite students. On a personal note, having grown up in Louisiana, I have seen how beneficial TOPS can be. The  scholarship can be the difference between just eking out a living 
during college to thriving. Additionally, the award is sometimes the deciding factor on whether or not some bright students attend college at all. However, because TOPS is a strictly 
merit-based scholarship, some have questioned whether recipients actually need the tuition waiver, preferring the money to go to other places. I believe that there, too, 
TOPS serves a purpose: if an individual achieves success due to Louisiana's K-12 investment, shouldn't the state try to retain that talent? Indeed, one core mission of TOPS 
is to "keep Louisiana’s best and brightest in the State to pursue postsecondary educational opportunities and become productive members of Louisiana’s workforce."

  The graphs above depicts the year-by-year TOPS payment made by Louisiana and the amount of TOPS recipients in each year. Immediately, what stands out is the big drop of tuition 
payouts in 2017. One would expect that the number of recipients should also experience a drop, but we actually have an increase in students receiving TOPS in 2017. This disparity is a
result of political gridlock between state legislators. In an effort to make up a growing state deficit, lawmakers decided to "frontload" the scholarship program. Effectively, this means 
that while students received full funding during the fall semester of 2016, the 2017 TOPS budget was sent into a free fall for those same students for the first time in the scholarship's history. 
The settled percentage was 41.8%, leaving scholars to close the gap on the full cost of tuition. 

<h2> How the Scholarship Works </h2>
  &emsp; Most TOPS recipient fall into one of three categories: Opportunity, Performance, or Honors. While all three awards cover the cost of tuition, the Performance and Honors award comes with 
a stipend. The assignment of awards is based off high school performance, which is shown in the below table:

In addition to the stipend, students are able to combine TOPS award with other sources financial aid sources, including Pell Grants or third party scholarships. This is not
uncommon, as the cost of attending university also includes room and board, textbooks, student life fees, health insurance, etc. Normally, the total grants, loan and scholarship
amount can not exceed the Cost of Attendance (COA); however, TOPS is a merit-based scholarship that will <b>always</b> pay the cost of tuition. If a student's
grants, loans, or scholarship already cover the cost, TOPS can push total aid to an excess amount, which triggers a refund. Although it is a point of contention, Louisiana's 
government provides assurance that "a student whose TOPS award is &hellip; refunded because of an over award of aid remains eligible for the award as long as the continuation requirements 
of the award are met." 

In practice, this means that some high-achieving students are <b>paid to go to college</b>, incentivizing them to remain in-state.
