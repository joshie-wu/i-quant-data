  &emsp; In 1989, the Louisiana Democratic caucus led by Charles R. McDonald teamed with Republican businessman Patrick Taylor to push the "Taylor Plan", or the Taylor Opportunity Program for Students (TOPS) as it is known today. The plan was simple: to make education more affordable for Louisiana's brightest by waiving tuition for high-achieving students. The hope was that intelligent high schoolers would become more intelligent college students (collegers?), who would later bring that intelligence into the Louisiana workforce. While TOPS works more or less the same today, the tapestry of Louisiana has changed significantly since the plan's inception.

<div class="callout">
  <p>“LSU took him [Taylor] in, didn’t charge him tuition, and gave him an education. That stuck with him, so he started helping young people financially.”</p>
  <p class = "credit"><b> Phyllis Taylor </b>, Patrick F. Taylor Foundation, Chairwoman; <i>2016</i></p>
</div>

For one, the scholarship is no longer funded by the Taylor Foundation. While states like Georgia fund their merit scholarships via the state's lottery system, TOPS is completely funded by Louisiana's Millennium Trust, costing around <b>300$ million</b> per year. This figure is only expected to rise in the next decade. With that price tag, many lawmakers have called into question the return on investment in Louisiana's brightest.[^1]

One critique of the program is its strict merit-based approach. By awarding TOPS to high-achieving students, Louisiana naturally awards individuals of higher socioeconomic status. From 2011 to 2021, the state has "paid tution for 11,000 students in the top income bracket." In percentages, 40% of TOPS recipipents come from the upper echelon, creating a question of equity. [^2]

<div class = "callout-two">
  <p>"We want to try something that no one in the country has done, which is to decouple student success with family income &hellip; because the correlation between family income and student success is so significant in a state that has as much poverty as Louisiana."</p>
  <p class="credit"><b>Dr. Kim Hunter Reid</b>, Louisiana Board of Regents, Commisioner of Higher Education; <i>2021</i> </p>
</div>

But, Louisiana does have a need-based scholarship program, called the GO Grant. Unlike TOPS, GO Grant is not funded by the Millenium Trust. Instead, the State of Louisiana's Office of Student Financial Assistance provides a yearly budget for the program. Additionally, while TOPS has constitutional safeguards about its operations, the GO Grant relies on year-to-year legislative discretions. As such, only <b>48% of qualified students</b> receive GO Grant need-based awards. [^3]

One thing seems strange: in 2017, there is a sharp decline in funding while the amount of TOPS recipients remained stable. As mentioned earlier, TOPS operational budget was codified into Louisianian law. That is true; however, the state faced a fiscal cliff in 2016 with a <b>1$ billion decrease</b> in revenue. With TOPS being a big budget item, legislators met to discuss cuts to the program. 

  The ultimate decision was to "frontload" payments, meaning that while students received full funding during the fall semester of 2016, the 2017 TOPS budget was sent into a free fall. The settled percentage was 41.8%, leaving those same scholars to close the gap on the full cost of tuition in the following semester. [^4]

<h2> How the Scholarship Works </h2>

  &emsp; Most TOPS recipient fall into one of three categories: Opportunity, Performance, or Honors. While all three awards cover the cost of tuition, the Performance and Honors award comes with a stipend. The assignment of awards is based off high school performance, which is shown in the below table:

In addition to the stipend, students are able to combine TOPS award with other sources financial aid sources, including Pell Grants or third party scholarships. This is common, as the cost of attending university also includes room and board, textbooks, student life fees, health insurance, etc. 

Normally, the total grants, loan and scholarship amount can not exceed the Cost of Attendance (COA); however, TOPS is a merit-based scholarship that will always pay the cost of tuition. If a student's financial aid package already cover the cost, TOPS can push total aid to an excess amount, which triggers a refund. Although it is a point of contention, Louisiana's 
government provides assurance that "a student whose TOPS award is &hellip; refunded because of an over award of aid remains eligible for the award as long as the continuation requirements of the award are met." 

In practice, this means that high-achieving students can be <b>paid to go to college</b>, incentivizing them to remain in-state. 

<h2> How Effective is TOPS? </h2> 

TOPS sets out to meet four goals: 

<ol>
  <li> Provide financial incentives for good academic performances </li>
  <li> Promote post-secondary education accessibility within the state </li>
  <li> Retain bright students within the state </li>
  <li> Encourage academic success through mandatory state curriculum and assessment testing (administered by the ACT) </li>
</ol>

Having previously discussed the first two points, we turn our attention to the latter two. How effective is TOPS in combatting brain drain, and have Louisiana high schoolers been motivated to meet TOPS requirements, especially given the rising cost of tuition across all post-secondary education systems.
